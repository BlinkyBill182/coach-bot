# Nutrition reference

Evidence for food advice. Position stands weigh most, then meta-analyses, then single trials. Every source here was checked against its abstract or full text (October 2026). "Practical" marks coaching judgement with no direct study behind it. This is coaching, not medical advice: blood tests, symptoms, medication and anything that feels wrong go to the user's doctor.

The numbers here are per kilo of body weight: use the weight in the log and the answers in the questionnaire. What the user doesn't eat, from the questionnaire and the saved facts, always holds.

Priorities: total calories and protein, every day, matter far more than timing, meal count or supplements.

## Energy (surplus and rate of gain)

**Sources:**
- **Aragon et al. 2017** (J Int Soc Sports Nutr; ISSN position stand on diets and body composition): gaining lean mass needs a sustained surplus. The surplus size and training status shape how much of the gain is muscle.
- **Iraki, Fitschen, Espinar, Helms 2019** (Sports; off-season bodybuilding review):
  - Novice and intermediate lifters: a surplus of about 10-20%, and a gain of about 0.25-0.5% of bodyweight per week.
  - Advanced lifters: about 5-10%, and about 0.25% per week.
- **Slater et al. 2019** (Front Nutr; review): the optimal surplus is unknown and untested in lifters. The authors advise a conservative surplus of about 1,500-2,000 kJ/day (about 360-480 kcal).
- **Helms et al. 2023** (Sports Med Open; RCT, 17 trained lifters finished, 8 weeks; maintenance vs a 5% vs a 15% surplus):
  - The groups did not differ in muscle thickness.
  - The 15% surplus raised skinfolds (body fat) more than maintenance.
  - Weight change predicted fat gain much better than muscle gain.
- **Garthe et al. 2013** (Eur J Sport Sci; 39 elite athletes, 8-12 weeks): a bigger counselled surplus gave +3.9% bodyweight vs +1.5%, and fat mass +15% vs +3%, with the same lean-mass gain.
- **Ribeiro et al. 2019** (J Hum Kinet; pilot, 11 bodybuilders, 4 weeks): the higher-calorie group gained a little more muscle (+2.7% vs +1.1%) and much more fat (+7.4% vs +0.8%).

**In practice:** for someone at 72 kg, 0.25-0.5% per week is about 0.18-0.36 kg. The SKILL.md goal of +0.15-0.25 kg/week sits at the lower, leaner end, which fits a lean bulk. The adjustment rules in SKILL.md stay:
- Under +0.1 kg for 3 weeks: add about 200 kcal a day.
- Over +0.4 kg per week: cut about 150 kcal a day.
- No calorie change before week 4, because creatine adds water early.
- Judge from the 7-day average, never from one weigh-in.

**Conflict check:** none. The evidence supports a small surplus, and larger surpluses mostly add fat.

## Estimating daily burn (the questionnaire)

Used once, to set a starting point; after about 4 weeks the weight trend beats it.

**Sources:**
- **Mifflin et al. 1990** (Am J Clin Nutr), resting metabolic rate: 10 x kg + 6.25 x cm - 5 x age, +5 for men, -161 for women.
- **Frankenfield, Roth-Yousey, Compher 2005** (J Am Diet Assoc; systematic review): Mifflin-St Jeor was the most reliable equation, within 10% of measured in more people than any other, with the narrowest error range. The authors warn of noteworthy errors in individuals. The share of people within 10% wasn't checked (abstract only).
- **2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities** (Herrmann et al., J Sport Health Sci; pacompendium.com): multi-exercise resistance training at 8-15 reps 3.5 METs; vigorous bodybuilding 6.0 METs (older-compendium wording, on the conditioning exercise page).
- **ACSM walking equation:** VO2 = 0.1 x m/min + 1.8 x m/min x grade + 3.5 (ml/kg/min), for about 1.9-4 mph. The flat, net part works out to about 0.5 kcal/kg/km (my derivation at 5 kcal per litre of oxygen).

**Practical (judgement, no direct study):**
- The day without the gym: BMR x 1.4 sitting, 1.55 mixed, 1.75 on the feet or physical work.
- A 75-minute lifting session: about 5 METs, so (5 - 1) x kg x 1.25 h, about 350 kcal at 70 kg.
- Goal: +10% to gain (the energy section above), +0 to keep, -20% to lose, never below BMR x 1.1 or 1,500 kcal for men and 1,200 for women.
- Protein 2.2 g/kg, fat 25% of calories and at least 0.9 g/kg, the rest carbs.
- A day without leaving home: about 4 km less walking x 0.5 kcal/kg/km, about 140 kcal, capped at 100-150. That's smaller than the error of the formula itself, so it's a nudge and never more than one day.

## Protein

**Daily amount:**
- **Jäger et al. 2017** (J Int Soc Sports Nutr; ISSN position stand on protein): 1.4-2.0 g/kg/day is enough for most people who train.
- **Morton et al. 2018** (Br J Sports Med; meta-analysis, 49 RCTs): gains in fat-free mass plateaued at about 1.62 g/kg/day (95% CI 1.03-2.20). The breakpoint was not statistically significant.
- **Thomas, Erdman, Burke 2016** (ACSM / Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics / Dietitians of Canada joint position): 1.2-2.0 g/kg/day for athletes.
- **In practice:** about 2.15 g/kg is at the top of the useful range. Days above that are harmless but add nothing for muscle, and they take room from carbs. When protein is already above about 2.2 g/kg, the next calories should be carbs.

**Per meal and spread:**
- **Jäger 2017:** 0.25 g/kg or 20-40 g of high-quality protein per serving, every 3-4 hours.
- **Kerksick et al. 2017** (J Int Soc Sports Nutr; ISSN position stand on nutrient timing): total daily protein comes first, then spacing.
- **Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018** (J Int Soc Sports Nutr): about 0.4 g/kg per meal over at least 4 meals, up to 0.55 g/kg per meal at the high end.
- **Trommelen et al. 2023** (Cell Rep Med; 36 young men): 100 g of protein after training gave a larger anabolic response than 25 g, lasting more than 12 hours. A big protein meal is not wasted; even spacing is a convenience, not a rule.
- **In practice:** 0.4-0.55 g/kg per meal, 4-5 times a day. This matches SKILL.md.

**After training and before sleep:**
- **Kerksick 2017:** high-quality protein from right after training up to about 2 hours later gives a strong rise in muscle protein synthesis. The size and timing of the meal before training change how much this matters.
- **Res et al. 2012** (Med Sci Sports Exerc; 16 young men, one night): 40 g of casein 30 min before sleep raised overnight whole-body protein synthesis and net protein balance. Muscle protein synthesis was about 22% higher, a borderline result.
- **Snijders et al. 2015** (J Nutr; 44 young men, 12 weeks): 27.5 g of protein plus 15 g of carbs every night before sleep gave more strength and quadriceps growth than placebo. The placebo group did not get the extra protein, so part of the effect is simply more daily protein.
- **Practical:** cottage cheese is mostly casein, so it's the closest match to the pre-sleep trials, which used casein. Other protein counts toward the day just the same.

**Quality of mixed protein powders (soy, whey and collagen):**
- **Messina et al. 2018** (Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab; meta-analysis, 9 studies): soy and whey or other animal proteins gave the same gains in strength and lean mass.
- **Oikawa et al. 2020** (Am J Clin Nutr; 22 older women): whey raised muscle protein synthesis; collagen did not over the longer term.
- **Practical:**
  - Collagen is an incomplete protein (it has no tryptophan) and has little leucine.
  - The collagen share of a powder counts toward daily protein but builds less muscle per gram.
  - A powder like that is fine as one of several protein sources. It shouldn't be the only protein in the post-workout meal or the pre-sleep snack; pair it with dairy, eggs, chicken or fish.

## Carbs and fat around morning training

**Carbs:**
- **Thomas, Erdman, Burke 2016:** 5-7 g/kg/day for moderate training (about 1 hour a day). About 5.3 g/kg on training days and 4.4 g/kg on rest days is reasonable for 60-90 min of lifting.
- **Kerksick 2017:** carbs eaten around resistance training support glycogen and reduce muscle damage.
- **Practical for a 09:00 session:**
  - The banana at 08:30 is a good pre-workout snack.
  - The half gainer after training brings carbs back fast.
  - The rest of the day's carbs can come in any order; the total matters most.

**Fat:**
- **Thomas, Erdman, Burke 2016:** fat should be 20-35% of energy. Staying at 20% or below brings no performance benefit.
- **Iraki 2019:** 0.5-1.5 g/kg/day.
- **Whittaker & Wu 2021** (J Steroid Biochem Mol Biol; meta-analysis, 6 studies, 206 men): low-fat diets lowered total and free testosterone modestly (SMD about -0.38). A 2026 corrigendum exists; its content wasn't checked.
- **In practice:** about 1.05-1.1 g/kg and 24-28% of energy sits within every range above. The floor in SKILL.md is about 0.9 g/kg.

**Fibre, fruit and vegetables:**
- **Reynolds et al. 2019** (Lancet; 185 cohort studies and 58 trials): the largest reductions in mortality, heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer came at 25-29 g of fibre a day, and higher may help more.
- **Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020-2025:** 14 g of fibre per 1,000 kcal. At 2,600-2,850 kcal that is about 36-40 g.
- **Aune et al. 2017** (Int J Epidemiol; 95 cohort studies): each 200 g a day of fruit and vegetables lowered all-cause mortality by about 10%, with benefits rising up to about 800 g a day.
- **In practice:** aim for at least 30 g of fibre and 5 or more portions of fruit and vegetables (400-800 g) a day. Easy sources: oats, potatoes and sweet potatoes with the skin, apples, bananas, berries, dates, vegetables, salad, and hummus or tahini.

## Hydration, sodium, alcohol, sleep

**Fluids:** Sawka et al. 2007 (Med Sci Sports Exerc; ACSM position stand on fluid replacement).
- Avoid losing more than 2% of body weight to sweat during a session.
- Weighing before and after a session shows the sweat loss.
- Replace fluid and salt afterwards.
- Practical: pale urine through the day is enough of a check. Creatine adds water inside the muscle; that is not dehydration.

**Sodium:** WHO 2012 guideline: under 2 g of sodium a day (5 g of salt) for the general adult population.
- Practical: heavy sweaters lose more sodium. Salting fresh home-cooked food is fine; the bulk of excess salt comes from processed food.

**Alcohol:** Parr et al. 2014 (PLoS One; 8 active men).
- About 12 drinks (1.5 g/kg) after training cut muscle protein synthesis by 24% even with 25 g of whey, and by 37% with carbs instead.
- Practical: one or two drinks on a rest day is a small cost; a big night out right after training is the worst case.

**Sleep:**
- **Lamon et al. 2021** (Physiol Rep; 13 young adults): one night without sleep cut muscle protein synthesis by 18% and testosterone by 24%, and raised cortisol by 21%.
- **Nedeltcheva et al. 2010** (Ann Intern Med; 10 adults, 14 days of dieting): 5.5 h vs 8.5 h in bed meant 55% less fat lost and 60% more lean mass lost.
- Practical: a short night costs more than a missed meal. Aim for 7.5-8 h.

## Supplements, graded

**Strong evidence:**
- **Creatine monohydrate.**
  - Kreider et al. 2017 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr; ISSN position stand): 3-5 g/day keeps muscle stores full. Weight gain is the only consistent side effect.
  - Antonio et al. 2021 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr): no kidney harm in healthy people.
  - Antonio et al. 2025 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr, part II): timing does not appear to matter; taking it every day does.
  - 5 g with a meal is right.
  - Hair: one trial (van der Merwe 2009, Clin J Sport Med) found DHT rose on creatine, but measured no hair loss. Most evidence finds no link.
  - Disclosure: authors of the ISSN creatine papers have ties to creatine makers.
- **Protein powder.** It is food, not a drug. Morton 2018: added protein gave +0.30 kg of fat-free mass overall, and more in trained lifters. Its job is to make the daily protein target easy.
- **Caffeine.** Guest et al. 2021 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr; ISSN position stand).
  - 3-6 mg/kg improves performance, taken about 60 min before training.
  - Half-life is usually 4-6 h. Late doses delay sleep and cut deep sleep.
  - Practical: coffee at wake, nothing after early afternoon.

**Good evidence, little relevance to building muscle:**
- Maughan et al. 2018 (Br J Sports Med; IOC consensus) lists caffeine, creatine, nitrate, sodium bicarbonate and possibly beta-alanine as having good evidence for performance.
- Beta-alanine (Trexler et al. 2015, J Int Soc Sports Nutr; ISSN position stand): 4-6 g/day helps efforts of 1-4 minutes. Its effect on strength is unclear, and it is not useful for hypertrophy.

**Weak or none:**
- **HMB:** an ISSN position stand claims benefits (Wilson et al. 2013), but the IOC 2018 consensus leaves it off the evidence-backed list.
- **BCAAs:** Wolfe 2017 (J Int Soc Sports Nutr): no benefit when total protein is adequate; whole protein already contains them.
- **Omega-3:**
  - Santo André et al. 2023 (Adv Nutr; 14 studies, healthy adults): no effect on muscle mass, and a very small one on strength.
  - VITAL (Manson et al. 2019, N Engl J Med; 25,871 adults): 1 g a day did not reduce major cardiovascular events overall.
  - Practical: fatty fish 2-3 times a week covers it.
- **Vitamin D:**
  - Beaudart et al. 2014 (J Clin Endocrinol Metab; 30 RCTs): a small strength gain, larger in deficient and older people, and no effect on muscle mass.
  - Endocrine Society 2024 (Demay et al., J Clin Endocrinol Metab): advises against doses above the dietary reference intake and against routine blood testing for healthy adults under 75.
- **High-dose vitamin C (and E):**
  - Paulsen et al. 2014 (J Physiol; 32 strength-trained adults, 10 weeks): 1,000 mg of vitamin C plus 235 mg of vitamin E a day did not change muscle growth, but blunted some strength gains and training signals.
  - Ristow et al. 2009 (PNAS): a similar dose blocked the gain in insulin sensitivity from training.
  - Practical: keep daily vitamin C at food levels and don't take antioxidant megadoses around training.
- **Emergen-C and colds:**
  - Hemilä & Chalker 2013 (Cochrane): regular vitamin C did not prevent colds in the general population. It did halve them under heavy physical stress (marathon runners, soldiers), and it shortened colds by about 8%. Taking it only once a cold started had no consistent effect.
  - Zinc lozenges over 75 mg a day shortened colds by about 33% (Hemilä 2017, JRSM Open). The Cochrane 2024 review (Nault et al.) rates this low certainty.
- **Contamination:** Maughan 2018: a survey found about 15% of more than 600 supplements had undeclared prohormones. Third-party testing lowers the risk but doesn't remove it.

## Health markers (not medical advice)

The user's doctor decides what to test. What the guidelines say, for context:
- **Testosterone:** Bhasin et al. 2018 (J Clin Endocrinol Metab; Endocrine Society guideline) and Mulhall et al. 2018 (J Urol; AUA guideline).
  - Measure on a fasting, early-morning sample, and confirm a low result with a repeat test on another morning.
  - The AUA cut-off is below 300 ng/dL. A diagnosis needs symptoms as well as low levels.
  - A bad night's sleep or a hard training week can lower one reading.
- **Heart risk from age 40:** USPSTF 2022 (Mangione et al., JAMA) recommends a statin for adults 40-75 with a risk factor and a 10-year risk of 10% or more. That assumes a lipid panel and a risk calculation are done at that age.
- **Creatine and kidney tests:** creatine turns into creatinine, so serum creatinine can rise without any kidney problem (Longobardi et al. 2023, Nutrients; Antonio 2021). Anyone taking creatine should tell the doctor, so a creatinine-based eGFR isn't misread.
- **Vitamin D:** the 2024 guideline advises against routine testing in healthy adults.
- **When to see a doctor:**
  - No progress after 8-12 weeks of a real surplus and consistent training (as SKILL.md says).
  - Unusual fatigue, low libido or low mood for weeks.
  - Chest pain or shortness of breath in training.
  - Blood in the urine, or anything else that worries them.

## Daily rules for the bot

1. Hit calories and protein first; everything else is fine-tuning.
2. Protein: about 0.4-0.55 g/kg in each of 4-5 meals. Above about 2.2 g/kg for the day, fill the rest with carbs, not more protein.
3. Training days: a carb snack before (banana), and carbs plus protein within about 2 hours after.
4. Fat at least about 0.9 g/kg; don't cut fat to make room for carbs.
5. Aim for at least 30 g of fibre and 5 portions of fruit and vegetables. When a day is low on both, suggest one easy fix (an apple, vegetables with dinner, oats).
6. Creatine 5 g every day, any time. A missed day doesn't matter; don't double up.
7. Coffee: at wake, not after early afternoon.
8. Alcohol: not on training evenings; if they drink, keep protein and sleep normal.
9. Sleep 7.5-8 h. After a short night, keep the plan and don't add calories to compensate.
10. Never suggest what the user doesn't eat. Prefer what's already cooked on the cooking days before suggesting something new.
11. For anything medical, say it is not medical advice and send them to their doctor.
